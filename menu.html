The idea, in one paragraph

Menu engineering is a method from 1982 (Kasavana and Smith, Michigan State) that most point-of-sale companies now teach. Put each item on two scales, what it earns per sale after ingredients and how often it sells, and it lands in one of four corners:

  • Star
    Sells well, earns well. Keep it exactly as it is and give it the best spot.
  • Plowhorse
    Sells well, earns little. Reprice it or trim the portion; a small change here is worth the most.
  • Puzzle
    Earns well, sells little. Move it, rename it, have staff mention it.
  • Dog
    Neither. Rework it or drop it.

Percent margin misleads here: a cupcake at 81% earns $3.44 a sale, a pie at 47% earns $9.44. Dollars a week decide. The sample report shows it on three bakery items with made-up numbers.

One client so far. In June 2026 I costed a home-based dessert business in the GTA: 46 recipes and 63 ingredients, in a workbook that recalculates itself when one ingredient price changes. The biggest finding was about where to bake: a rented commercial kitchen would add $13.33 to the cost of every unit, enough to erase a home-production margin of about 76%. I also replaced one flat $15 price, which undercharged for the premium items, with a retail and a wholesale price for each product. The business is not named here, and none of its data is shown.
